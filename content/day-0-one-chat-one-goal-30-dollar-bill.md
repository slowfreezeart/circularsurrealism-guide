Most "how I built it" stories start with the finished product. This series starts on the day the work was still a plan.

**Day 0 was Sunday, 25 October 2026.** Slowfreeze, an AI artist who is not a developer, opened a first chat with a Grok Bot he named Consu. He had a finished site, but no team, no guide and no store. This article is the honest version of that first day, and of where the numbers stand now, including the ones that are still zero.

Today, 13 November, is Day 19.

## What did Slowfreeze already have on Day 0?

One finished AI Art site and one very long chat.

[circularsurrealism.com](https://circularsurrealism.com) is an AI Art museum-style site. It now shows 440+ works in 27 collections, with live sales data across chains, a print shop and a read-only wallet gallery. Slowfreeze vibe coded all of it in Grok Build, in a single conversation. The first prompt was on **27 August 2026**. The last big pieces landed in October. In total it took about 8 weeks and 400+ prompts.

The site was the case study. The Day 0 question was whether the story of building it could become something useful to other people.

## What was the first question on Day 0?

Not "write me a guide." The first real question was about teams.

Slowfreeze asked whether bots work separately, or whether a bot like Consu and another bot he might create could work together on one objective. Consu's answer: yes. Each bot has its own chat, memory and personality, so you can give each one a clear role. They all share the same computer, so files, tools and logins one bot sets up are there for the others. One thing they do not share is a screen, so two can browse at the same time.

At that point there was exactly one bot. Consu saw no other bots on the account yet. The team came later the same day and gets its own article.

Consu also opened circularsurrealism.com and described what it saw: the orbs and knights, the live sales marks, the prints and the wallet gallery. It could not see the Grok Build chat itself, so the article and repo became its source material.

## What was the plan?

Slowfreeze's plan had three parts:

1. Take his long build article (it reads in about 41 minutes), the repo files and the build chat.
2. Turn them into a step-by-step course or tutorial, with recommendations and ways to solve the problems a builder will hit.
3. Sell it at a price that is hard to resist, and let a team of bots work on selling it.

The first goal was small and specific. He wanted the sales to cover his $30 a month Grok subscription, which started on 25 October 2026, before aiming for anything bigger.

The build itself had run on a cheaper plan. The build diary records that it ran on the $11-a-month X plan at the time, plus at least $75 in $5 credit packs. So the $30 a month subscription is the plan the team runs on, not the plan the site was built on.

## What did Consu come back with on Day 0?

A blueprint, a break-even and two checks.

**The blueprint.** The first course blueprint had 13 modules and 59 lessons, each tied to a part of the article or a file in the repo, with a common-problems section at the end of every module. It was only an outline. It floated three price ideas: $39 (or $29 at launch), $79 and $199.

**The break-even.** Against a $30 a month goal, the math is short: one sale at $39 or two sales at $19 each month covers it before fees. Consu also noted that Gumroad takes a fee, leaving about $16.60 on a $19 sale.

**Check one: secrets.** Before anything was shared, Consu went through the repo for passwords and keys. It found no real passwords or private keys. It did find two hardcoded IDs that would need to come out before any template was shared, and it noted that wallets, email and the catalog should stay out of anything public.

**Check two: gating.** Slowfreeze wanted to lock the original article eventually. Consu pointed out that the full text ships inside the page code, so hiding it on screen is not enough. It has to be removed from the public page.

## How did the plan change during the day?

It got bigger, and then it got scoped down.

Consu moved to writing the full lessons. The blueprint's 59 lessons became a 102-lesson plan the same day, with visuals, two PDFs, a free checklist to give away for email signups, and the store listing text. The $55 tier was put on hold, so the first launch would be two paid guides: a $19 Quick Start and a $39 House Guide, plus the free checklist.

On visuals, the decision was real screenshots of the site with numbered notes, plus simple diagrams for the harder ideas. Every visual had to teach something.

Consu needed one thing from Slowfreeze, a free Gumroad account, and nothing would go live until he gave it a final look. That approval rule runs through the whole series.

Later that night, at 11:38 PM CT, the three Gumroad products went live. How that launch worked is its own article. The guide has since grown to 21 modules and 114 lessons, but Day 0 started with 59.

## What are the real numbers after 19 days?

Small, and sourced. These are the only results this article claims.

- **Gumroad totals so far (13 November):** one download, the free checklist, and no paid sales.
- **Product page views:** 6 in the 30 days to 26 October, 5 from direct, email or chat links and 1 from X.
- **First download:** on **12 November 2026, Day 18**, that one download came in. It was the free [Performance Checklist](https://slowfreezebuilds.gumroad.com/l/svpmd), and the referrer was guide.circularsurrealism.com, the free article hub. A free download is not a sale, but it is the first proof that hub readers reach the store.
- **Followers:** @Slowfreeze98 went from 3,055 on 27 October to 3,070 on 2 November. Slowfreeze's own estimate on 13 November is about 5,200 followers across all networks.

So the $30 a month bill is not covered yet. Saying so plainly is the point of the series. The hub and the X Articles are free on purpose, to earn through views and audience while pointing readers to the guide.

## What can you take from Day 0?

If you have a finished project and want to turn it into something you can teach, these are the Day 0 steps that held up:

1. **Start from a real artifact.** The article, the repo and the build chat were facts to draw on.
2. **Name one small money goal.** "Cover the monthly bill" is easier to measure than "make a living."
3. **Check for secrets first.** Passwords, keys and IDs in a repo are a Day 0 problem.
4. **Keep the first launch small.** Two paid guides and one free download beat a long menu.
5. **Report the zeros.** Numbers you can verify are worth more than a good-looking story.
